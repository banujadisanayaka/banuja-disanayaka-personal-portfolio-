PROJECT JOURNAL

TFC Orders Tracker

2026 / Operations → productExplore the system ↓

01 / THE CONTEXT

From an order on a phone to a coordinated kitchen.

My starting point was practical experience at The Food Company in Kuala Lumpur. As an Outlet PIC / Operations Executive and assistant in ordering administration, I worked around stock, suppliers, production, POS closing and orders arriving through different channels. That experience shaped the questions behind the product: what is still needed, who is working on it, and what can actually leave the kitchen?

A list of orders alone cannot answer those questions. One item may be cooking while another is ready to pack. A shortage changes the plan. Several orders may need the same product. The product therefore follows the work at item level and connects it back to the larger order and production plan.

The design question

When orders arrive through retail, wholesale and delivery channels, the difficult part is keeping the whole operation aligned. An order is only useful when the people preparing, checking and handing it over share the same picture.

02 / THE WORKFLOW

Follow the system.

Explore each stage to see how the parts connect.

01

Order → shared queue

Capture the demand

An administrator creates an order with a kitchen, date and product quantities. Items can be entered individually or staged from a pasted message using the documented bulk parser. The important handoff is from an informal request into a structured order that the relevant teams can see.

Product matching supports entry; a person still needs to check the staged items. Parsing a message is a convenience, not proof that its contents are correct.

02

Plan → recipe → batch

Coordinate production

The daily facility plan brings together products, quantities and linked recipes. Production staff can see ingredients and preparation instructions alongside the work. When several orders require the same product, the documented master-batch workflow groups that production demand.

Completing a linked batch updates the associated order items to the next preparation state. The model keeps the relationship between shared production and individual orders visible.

03

Cooking → ready → packed

Make the handoff visible

Packing staff work with individual item states: send an item to production, mark it packed, or record a shortage or out-of-stock issue. Those distinctions explain what is happening much more clearly than a single complete/incomplete flag on the entire order.

The product separates preparing food from packing and dispatching it. A completed production task should not be confused with a completed delivery.

04

Dispatch → status update

Close the communication loop

Once the relevant items are packed, dispatch is confirmed in the workflow. A formatted WhatsApp status export helps communicate the order state, including items still in production and outstanding issues, using the information already recorded by the team.

Firebase listeners support updates across connected interfaces. Network conditions and correct staff input still matter; the portfolio does not claim zero latency or the removal of all manual coordination.

03 / THE EXPERIENCE

What the product needs to make clear.

01

Different views for different work

The documented roles cover administration, packing, production and the two client-kitchen views. Role selection and approval let the interface follow a person’s responsibility instead of showing every control to everyone.

02

Exceptions are part of the workflow

Short stock, unavailable items, notes and quantity changes belong beside the order. Bringing exceptions into the shared record helps explain why an item has not moved forward.

03

A day-level view as well as an order view

Production planning and the dashboard provide a wider perspective. The order remains useful for an individual handoff, while the daily plan helps organise work across orders.

04 / UNDER THE SURFACE

How the architecture supports the idea.

React · TypeScript · Firebase · Netlify

React + TypeScript

The application presents the role-specific workspaces and the order, production and packing interactions. A shared interface model keeps those views connected.

Firebase

Shared application records and listeners support the live workflow. Authentication and access configuration are part of the deployment, not a substitute for testing the data rules.

Product and recipe structure

Products, quantities and recipe references give the plan operational meaning. The public case study explains that structure without exposing business records or recipe contents.

Netlify and supporting documentation

The repository includes a Netlify function, a detailed V8 user guide and visual checks. The guide explains the handoffs a team member needs to understand when using the tool.

05 / THE NEXT CHAPTER

What matters from here.

The key product decision is to model responsibility and state together. A beautiful dashboard means little if the packing team and the production team interpret “done” differently. Clear status language and a visible next action are central to this project.

The next useful validation is an observed operational trial: check how staff handle interrupted connections, corrections, shared devices and busy dispatch periods. Measured impact should come from that work, rather than from a percentage added to a portfolio card.

Where it stands

This case study describes the implemented architecture and the workflow behind it. It does not claim measured time savings, verified adoption figures or an independent production audit.
